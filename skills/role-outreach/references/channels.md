# The five channels

Choosing the channel is the judgment that matters most here, and it has no deterministic
version because it depends on things only reading reveals: whether a warm path exists,
whether the company's work is public enough to engage with, and whether the operator can
*show* something rather than claim it.

The default failure is reaching for cold email because it is the channel that scales.
Scaling is the wrong objective. A record of processes that reach the final round and then
stall describes a funnel that loses at the end. More volume at the top does not touch
that. Arriving with a person attached does.

Channels are listed by yield, not by convenience.

---

## 1. VC talent partner

**When:** the company is fund-backed and the operator has any real tie to the fund.

**List every tie the operator actually has before reading further**, across all their
areas, ranked by strength: an advisor with a company of their own, a professor who is
chief scientist somewhere, a fund programme with portfolio access. Keep it in
`state/warm-paths.md`. Record where no warm path exists too, and what substitutes there;
a whole area with no fund tie at all is common.

Covered in full in `vc-talent.md`, and it goes first for a reason: talent partners are the
only people in this whole process whose actual job is to place the operator. Everyone else
is doing them a favour.

Look for a tie that already exists and is not being used: a programme the fund runs, a
portfolio company the operator worked at, a partner who has seen the work. A **fund
programme** is not a line on a résumé. It is often membership in a network with a staffed
talent function whose purpose is placing its members into portfolio companies.

**What it is not:** asking for a job. That is a category error and it burns the
relationship. You tell a talent partner what you are looking for — area, role shape,
timing, location — and let them match it against portfolio openings they already know
about. See `vc-talent.md` for the register and the trap.

## 2. Referral through the network

**When:** anyone from the operator's lab, cohort, teaching or past teams is already inside.

Highest yield of anything cold-adjacent, and the cheapest to check. A referral is not a
favour asked of a stranger; it is a favour asked of someone who already has a view of the
operator's work.

Where to look, roughly in order of how well they know the work:

- **The operator's research groups** — labs place people into exactly these companies, and
  a current or former labmate at the target is the strongest possible path. An advisor
  with industry ties reaches further still.
- **Small cohorts** — an honours programme or a selective scholarship cohort is small, and
  a few years on its members are at precisely the seniority that can refer.
- **Teaching** — a few semesters as a TA is several hundred students, many now at target
  companies, all of whom remember a TA who was good.
- **Prior employers** — colleagues move, and a former manager is a reference and a
  referral in one.
- **A fund fellowship's cohort** — peers, distinct from the fund's talent team.

**The ask that works** is specific and small: not "can you refer me", which asks someone to
vouch before they know what for, but "I'm applying for X at your company — is that team any
good, and would you be willing to drop my name in?" The first half gives them something to
answer even if the second half is a no.

**What a rule gets wrong here:** treating a shared institution as a warm path. An alum of
the operator's school who has never met them is a cold contact with a slightly better
opening line. Warmth comes from someone having seen the work, not from a shared
affiliation.

---

## 3. Work-first

**When:** the company has a public repo, benchmark, model or paper the operator can
actually engage with. Slow, and it converts better than anything else that starts cold.

Instead of writing about wanting to work there, do a piece of the work and let it start the
conversation: a real pull request, a reproduction of a result with the failure cases
written up, a benchmark run on their released model, an issue that demonstrates you read
the code.

It is a strong fit when the operator's artifacts are already this shape. A library that
reproduces a company's released model is not a claim about being interested in their
work, it is having already trained their architecture. That email does not need a hook.

**The cost is real:** days, not minutes, and it does not parallelise. Spend it on the
handful of companies that scored `strong` on ten-year value, not on the list.

**What a rule gets wrong:** treating a drive-by PR as work-first. A typo fix in a README
signals nothing except that you can find a README. The engagement has to be substantial
enough that the maintainer had to think about it.

## 4. Direct email to the role owner

**When:** under roughly 300 people, a named owner, and a real thesis to lead with — **and
nobody at that domain has heard from the operator in the last month.**

**Check prior contact before writing a single line of this channel.** Any earlier campaign
from the operator's mailbox probably reached the same sectors, and research will happily
rank someone already written to as the highest-leverage person in a sector. The check is
one search of the Sent folder, for the domain and for the person's name, recorded on the
outreach record. Not checking costs the relationship.

Search for the name as well as the domain, because of the trap: a founder written to at
their company address can resurface under a personal domain, and a search for the domain
alone clears them. **Same person, second address, and it reads worse than a duplicate.**
Check the person, not just the string.

Moderate yield, and genuinely good under fifty people, where the founder reads their own
mail.

Everything that makes it work is in the first two sentences: a specific claim about their
hardest problem, and the artifact that backs it. Everything that makes it fail is
generic — and a generic note at a forty-person company is permanent, because the founder
remembers and there is no second first impression.

**The pool to start from, before sourcing anyone new:** founders, CTOs, chief scientists
and PIs that the operator's own earlier research already found and nobody has written to.
This channel wants leadership, and their addresses are often already observed. Reuse only
what the operator gathered for themselves: research done for a club's or an employer's
campaign belongs to that campaign, even when its mail went out from the operator's
mailbox. Keep the list in `state/`. Cap at one person per company.

**The bar to clear before writing:** can you name the team's hardest current problem, and
write one sentence connecting an artifact to it? If not, that is not a fit, and not writing
is the correct outcome. The target record from `role-sourcing` carries this as `thesis`; if
it came back `none`, do not route this company to email.

## 5. The application form

**Always, in addition. Never instead.**

Low yield alone — it is the black hole most new-grad funnels already lose in. But it is
not optional: it is how the company creates a record, and a hiring manager who wants to
move you forward needs one to point at.

**Timing is the whole trick.** Submit the same day the email goes out, never before and
never long after. The email is what causes the application to be read; an application
sitting alone for three weeks has already been sorted.

**What a rule gets wrong:** treating submission as progress. It is bookkeeping. If a week's
work is forty applications and no conversations, that week produced nothing.

## Choosing, in practice

**Every company gets scored on every channel, not routed to one.** This corrects an earlier
version of this file which said "ask in this order and stop at the first yes". That rule
produced single-verdict thinking, where one dead channel got written up as the company
being dead. A company can be a scratch on email and still alive on cold apply: attack each
company several ways.

A channel closing tells you about **that channel**, not about the company. The four questions
are independent and a company can be alive on two and dead on two:

| channel | the question | what a "no" actually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cold apply** | is there a posting the operator is eligible for? | only that *today's board* has nothing. Boards turn over; re-check. |
| **Email someone inside** | is there a named person with public work to engage, **and is the domain clean of earlier outreach?** | the people are unreachable, unidentified, or already written to this month — none of which says the company won't hire |
| **VC talent** | is a fund with a talent function on the cap table? | the fund has no platform layer, or no tie exists. Says nothing about the company. |
| **Work-first / project** | is there a public model, benchmark, dataset or paper to build on? | rarely a real no at a company that publishes |

**Worked example.** A company can be dead on *email* as a first move, because its people
were written to last month, and dead on *cold apply for research roles*, because every
technical requisition asks for a PhD, or an MS plus three to five years. It is still
alive on **work-first** if its research arm publishes openly; alive on **cold apply
later**, because boards turn over and an open-application form exists; and alive on **VC
talent**, through the funds on its cap table. Calling it "a scratch" is wrong: it is a
scratch *on one channel this month*.

## Sequencing, which is a separate question from selection

Score every channel. **Then sequence them** — that is where the old rule was right, and the
reason survives: a talent-partner intro and a cold email landing in the same week makes the
operator look uncoordinated to two people who speak to each other.

1. **Work-first first when it exists**, because it takes longest to mature and it makes every
   later channel stronger. A project shipped in October is what the December email is about.
2. **Warm paths before cold ones** — VC talent and referral before direct email.
3. **The application goes in the same day the email does**, never before and never instead.
4. **Wait two weeks for a channel to actually fail** before escalating to the next.
5. **Cap at 2 people per company, 1 under fifty headcount** — that is counting, not judgment,
   and it is the number that drifts upward under enthusiasm.

**Record the per-channel state on the target**, not one verdict. A company approached through
the wrong channel and gone quiet is not the same as a company that is not interested, and
only the record can tell them apart later.
