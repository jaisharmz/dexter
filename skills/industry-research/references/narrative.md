# How the field got here

Every run produces an essay, `narrative.md`, telling the intellectual history of the field: how it started, who argued with whom, which ideas descend from which, what died, and what is still unresolved.

**Target 1,500 to 2,500 words.** This is the longest single piece of prose the run produces and it should be. A reader finishing it should be able to hold a conversation with someone who works in the field, place any new paper in a lineage, and understand why the branches on the map exist rather than merely that they do.

It is not a summary and it is not a timeline. `landscape.md` holds the dated events. This is the argument those events were about.

## What it has to do

A reader arrives knowing a few names and a few results, in no particular order. They leave understanding the field as a sequence of positions people took under uncertainty, most of which looked reasonable at the time and some of which were wrong.

Three tests, and an essay that fails any of them needs another pass:

Could the reader, given a paper from this field they have never seen, guess roughly which camp it comes from and what it is arguing against?

Could they explain to someone else why two subfields that look similar do not talk to each other?

Do they know which currently-confident position is most likely to look foolish in three years, and why?

## Go back further than the hype

The most common failure is starting the story at the current funding cycle. Almost every field has roots two or three decades older than its present attention, and those roots are usually where the concepts came from.

Find the earlier lineage and trace it forward. Which of today's ideas is a rediscovery? Which was proposed once, failed for reasons that no longer apply, and came back when the constraint lifted? Which vocabulary was borrowed from an older discipline, and does the borrowing still hold?

That deeper root is most of the difference between an essay and a news summary. A reader who knows that today's argument is a fifty-year-old argument with better compute understands something a reader who started in 2023 cannot.

## Structure

Organize by argument, not by year. A chronology is what `landscape.md` already provides, and reading a story in date order flattens the causation that makes it a story.

A workable shape, though not a template to follow slavishly:

**Origins.** Where the ideas actually came from, including the pre-history most write-ups skip.

**The founding disagreement.** The claim that organized the modern field and the credible objection to it. Both sides stated fairly, with the strongest version of each rather than the version its opponents use.

**The branches.** How that disagreement produced the subfields on the map. This is where a reader learns why the map has the shape it does, and it is the most valuable section.

**The collisions.** Where a technique or a person crossed between branches, and what changed as a result. Fields advance at these seams more often than inside any one branch.

**Where money entered.** Not a funding recap, but the places where capital changed what got worked on. A well-funded wrong turn is a real event in an intellectual history.

**The graveyard.** What was mainstream three years ago and is now abandoned, and why. Give this real space.

**The live argument.** Where it stands, what is genuinely unsettled, and what evidence would settle it.

Sections need titles a reader can navigate by, and the essay should be readable in pieces.

## Anchoring

Every claim about what someone believed attaches to a dated artifact: a paper, a talk, a post, a founding announcement, a resignation. Link it inline.

"He disagreed with the scaling view" is a vibe. "He said so in this talk in 2022, and founded the lab pursuing the alternative four years later" is history.

Where a position is inferred rather than stated, say so. "His published work implies he thinks X, though I could not find him saying it" is honest and still useful. Presenting an unstated belief as a quote is not.

Quote sparingly and exactly. One good sentence in someone's own words is worth a paragraph of paraphrase, and a misquote in an essay a reader repeats out loud is the worst failure available here.

## Trace descent explicitly

Where an idea extends an earlier one, say which and how. This is what readers value most and what most write-ups skip, because it requires knowing two literatures rather than one.

A chain of three is worth more than three separate summaries. Naming that A came out of B, which was an application of C from a neighbouring field, is often the single most clarifying sentence available about a research area.

Where two lines converged, say what each brought. Where one absorbed the other, say who conceded.

## Six ways this goes wrong

**Great-man history.** Attributing a field to four famous people when two hundred did the work. Senior names are load-bearing for the *argument*, since they are the ones who state positions publicly, but say plainly when a result came from a group, a lab, or a community. Where a graduate student did the work and the advisor's name is attached, that is worth a clause.

**Hindsight determinism.** Writing so the present looks inevitable. It was not. At every fork credible people took the other side for real reasons, and if the essay cannot say what those reasons were, it is propaganda for the winner.

**Hagiography.** Researchers as visionaries who saw the future. They made bets under uncertainty and some paid off. Say "bet," not "saw."

**Dropping the losers.** The abandoned branch is often the most informative part, because it tells the reader what has been tried. An essay containing only the winning line teaches someone to repeat a mistake already made.

**Positions without evidence.** Any sentence of the form "X believes Y" needs X saying Y, linked. This is where an essay quietly becomes fiction.

**The tidy ending.** Fields do not resolve. An essay that closes with the current approach triumphant has been tidied, and the reader is about to walk into an argument the essay told them was over.

## Register

Same voice as everything else, per `voice.md`. Past tense, active, named actors doing things.

Do not open with "the story of this field begins." Start at the first real claim.

Do not tell the reader the story is fascinating or the field is fast-moving. If it is interesting, it will read as interesting, and a sentence asserting so is evidence it does not.

Have a view. Say who turned out right where there is enough evidence, say when there is not, and say which current position you would bet against. A history that declines to judge anything reads as evasive, and part of why the reader is asking is that they want your read.

## Where it goes

`narrative.md` holds the essay. `README.md` opens with a compressed version, about 150 words, that ends by pointing at the file. The report page renders the full essay in its own section, above everything else, because it is what makes the map underneath legible.
